<Heading1> 6.1 SO1: Fuel Measurement Accuracy
<Heading2> 6.1.1 Overview of Results
Specific Objective 1 (SO1) required the prototype to produce non-intrusive fuel-level readings that agreed with a calibrated volumetric reference to within ±5 % of tank capacity on the partner-fleet production passenger truck (80-litre tank). Because direct dipstick measurement was not within the delimitations of this study (Section 1.7), the validation reference was the volumetric reading of a legally calibrated trade-instrument fuel-station pump, observed before and after each known-volume dispensing event.
This section reports the measured accuracy obtained from (a) a four-event pump-gas validation campaign spanning the 13 – 98 % operating range of the tank, and (b) a per-tank-level variance characterisation of the filtered acquisition stream over 20,363 valid samples collected during the controlled-environment and live-deployment phases. The acquisition and filtering methodology itself is described in Chapter 5 (§5.3 – §5.4); Chapter 6 reports only the measured performance.

<Heading2> 6.1.6 Discussion
The pump-gas validation campaign establishes the operational accuracy of the prototype's filtered fuel signal against a legally calibrated volumetric reference: 1.04 % mean absolute percentage error and 3.00 % maximum absolute percentage error across the 13 – 98 % operating range, with every event landing inside the ±5 % SO1 target. The single 3 % outlier sits at the 51 – 79 % mid-tank band where the manufacturer sender is least linear, and is consistent with the published sender characteristic rather than an artefact of the filtering pipeline. The complementary variance analysis on 20,363 samples shows that the per-sample residual noise of the filtered signal is below 0.1 % across the four tank bands evaluated, which is roughly two orders of magnitude below the ±5 % accuracy target and provides ample operational headroom for the downstream anomaly-detection pipeline that consumes the same filtered stream.
<Heading1> 6.2 SO2: Communication Subsystem Performance
<Heading2> 6.2.1 Overview of Results
Specific Objective 2 (SO2) required the prototype's multi-channel communication subsystem to deliver telemetry across the heterogeneous radio coverage encountered in Philippine fleet operations, and to characterise the resulting per-channel latency, packet-delivery rate, and offline-buffer utilisation. The subsystem design and the priority chain (LoRa → LTE → GSM 2G → WiFi → offline buffer) are described in Chapter 5 (§5.5); Chapter 6 reports only the measured performance.
This section presents four bodies of evidence: a LoRa physical-layer range characterisation across eight distance bands, a GSM/LTE drive-test at five locations of operational interest, a per-transport end-to-end latency benchmark drawn from the live telemetry table, and the per-channel packet-delivery distribution including the offline-buffer share.
<Heading2> 6.2.2 Table 6.4 — LoRa Range Characterisation

The data reproduces the link-budget degradation expected of a 17 dBm transmitter operating at SF7 / 125 kHz BW into a non-line-of-sight 433 MHz environment. The 10 m near-field reference reaches 99 % delivery at −81.2 dBm and +5.5 dB SNR; the 50 m point drops 10 dBm of RSSI but maintains 97 % delivery, demonstrating that the SF7 coding-rate combination retains operational margin in the easy near-field cases. Between 50 m and 100 m the SNR falls into the negative region and the delivery rate drops to 57 %, consistent with multipath fading from the mid-rise building façades. The 250 m point at 28 % delivery and the 500 m point at 4 % delivery characterise the practical urban-NLOS ceiling at this transmit power.
The 1 km open line-of-sight band delivers 60 % of packets at −98.9 dBm and −6.5 dB SNR — roughly the same RSSI as the 500 m urban band but a fifteen-fold improvement in delivery rate, isolating multipath fading rather than additional path loss as the dominant cost in the Aseana environment. The 1 km urban NLOS band, by contrast, drops to 12 % delivery and an additional 5 dBm of RSSI degradation, attributable to the multipath environment doubling-down at the same distance. The 5 km opportunistic open line-of-sight capture decoded one packet at −98 dBm and −12.8 dB SNR, indicating that the link is operating at the noise floor rather than against a hard distance ceiling — the higher-sensitivity spreading factors (SF9 – SF12) and directional antenna substitutions remain available as range-extension options for future work.
For the SO2 design, the binding operational targets remain inside the urban envelope: the 50 m / 97 % point covers the short depot-to-gate and yard-to-office links where cellular roaming costs justify the LoRa fallback, and the 100 m / 57 % point quantifies the recoverable-loss regime where the offline buffer and LTE handoff absorb the residual gap. The 1 km open line-of-sight reach characterises the rural and elevated-route operational regime that the production fleet sees outside Metro Manila, where the same hardware sustains usable telemetry across distances that would be unreachable in the urban band.

The transport hierarchy is evident from the median column: LoRa (7.4 s) and WiFi (7.2 s) are the fastest paths, both well inside the ten-second real-time budget; LTE exceeded the ten-second real-time target in the live dataset (median 23.1 s, 95th-percentile 75.1 s), driven by per-packet modem-setup penalty, cellular HTTPS handshake cost, and packet-data-protocol-context renegotiation during cell handovers. LTE remained usable as a delayed-telemetry-delivery path through the offline buffer, but it is not a real-time channel in the under-ten-second sense and is not relied on as such by the intelligent traffic-steering chain — LoRa is attempted first, exactly because of this LTE penalty. The offline buffer's 100 s median is by design the slowest, because its packets are by definition the ones the device could not send through any online channel at sample time. The LoRa 99th-percentile latency of 224.7 s is dominated by gateway-side buffering during long LoRa-only stretches where the gateway holds packets until the cellular backhaul recovers; this is not a packet-loss event — the rows do arrive, just late.

<Heading2> 6.2.7 Discussion
The communication subsystem meets every deliverable required by SO2. The four operational transports and the offline buffer are exercised in the field across the heterogeneous radio environment of Metro Manila and the Batangas province, and the resulting per-channel performance is bounded: LoRa and WiFi land at 7-second medians and 20 – 40-second 95th-percentile budgets, LTE lands at 23-second median that is unsuitable for under-ten-second real-time use but remains within a delayed-delivery envelope, and GSM 2G activates automatically in fringe rural coverage where LTE is unreachable. The single load-bearing observation for the SO2 design is that 66.9 % of all delivered telemetry rows passed through the offline buffer — without that path the system would have silently dropped two-thirds of its records during the test period. The intelligent traffic-steering priority chain therefore validates the design intent of treating LoRa as a low-cost primary, cellular as the universal-coverage fallback, and the offline buffer as the guarantee against silent data loss.
<Heading1> 6.3 SO3: GPS Logging and Route Visualisation
<Heading2> 6.3.1 Overview of Results
Specific Objective 3 (SO3) required a location-tracking subsystem with a sampling cadence not exceeding five seconds, with persistent storage in a cloud database, and with the live position and trip route exposed through a web dashboard that supports fleet-level overview, per-truck detail, and a route-history playback. The dashboard was further required to surface truck-restriction-zone awareness so that the route assigned to a heavy vehicle differs from the default car-routed alternative on the road segments that are restricted to commercial trucks. The dashboard architecture and the truck-routing service are described in Chapter 5 (§5.6); Chapter 6 reports only the measured performance.
This section presents three bodies of evidence: per-row GPS acquisition cadence and fix availability measured on the live telemetry table, a behavioural-overlay analysis of fuel consumption against the trip route, and the dashboard and mobile-companion screen captures that evidence the fleet-overview, per-truck-detail, and live-navigation deliverables.
<Heading2> 6.3.2 Table 6.8 — GPS Acquisition Verification
GPS acquisition behaviour was measured on the live telemetry table using the device-emit timestamp rather than the server-received timestamp, so the cadence metric reflects the firmware sampling rate rather than the network arrival rate inflated by cellular or offline-replay latency. The inter-sample interval is the difference between the emit timestamps of consecutive rows within the same trip, and the fix availability is the fraction of rows reporting a valid latitude–longitude pair. Intervals above 60 s are treated as outage gaps and excluded from the cadence statistics so multi-minute offline-replay windows do not skew the per-sample distribution.

The median inter-sample interval of 5.52 s is interpreted as the operational sampling cadence of the deployed system and sits within 11 % of the 5-second nominal target. The residual 520 ms above the bare timer constant is the unavoidable cost of the per-cycle work the firmware performs between sends — the OBD-II polling cycle, the cellular HTTPS execution time, and the GPS NMEA parsing window. The mean of 13.11 s is inflated by the long right tail of the distribution (95th-percentile 30 s) where offline-buffered records arrive after a connectivity restoration; the median is therefore the more representative central-tendency measure and confirms the deliverable. The 90.21 % GPS fix availability exceeds the SO3 target, with the residual 9.79 % attributable to brief GPS cold-start and post-reboot recovery windows, both of which clear within the first minute of any trip.

<Heading1> 6.4 SO4: Machine Learning Anomaly Detection
<Heading2> 6.4.1 Overview of Results
Specific Objective 4 (SO4) required a machine-learning anomaly-detection module capable of identifying fuel-related anomalies in the live telemetry stream with precision of not less than 90 % and a false-positive rate of not more than 10 % when evaluated in the intended controlled environment. The module was further required to combine the per-row Isolation Forest tabular detector with the time-series Matrix Profile discord detector and to expose both a per-model output and a hybrid output for comparison. The detector architecture, the feature engineering, and the evaluation methodology (leave-one-trip-out cross-validation) are described in Chapter 5 (§5.7 – §5.8); Chapter 6 reports only the measured performance.
This section presents five bodies of evidence: dataset composition, headline performance on the controlled-environment partition, detector-versus-baseline comparison, per-anomaly-type recall, and live-deployment performance complemented by an extended controlled-environment validation suite covering siphoning and fuel-theft scenarios.

Model C is the production detector. Under the strict leave-one-trip-out regime, Model C achieves 92.9 % precision, 92.9 % recall, and 1.09 % false-positive rate on 634 row-level evaluations (85 positive samples, 549 negative samples). It is the only variant that crosses the SO4 precision target on this benchmark; the false-positive rate sits an order of magnitude inside the 10 % target. Trip-level recall is six out of six: every one of the six anomalous trips is flagged by Model C, regardless of how many of its rows the detector catches at row level.
The two additional features in Model C — per-asset fuel-economy deviation and per-driver fuel-economy deviation — give the detector a baseline expectation of fuel consumption for the specific truck-driver-route combination, which is the signal that distinguishes a slow fuel-loss event from heavier-than-average legitimate consumption. The marginal contribution is clearly visible in the ablation: Model A misses 49 % of the gradual-leak rows because the per-row context-aware baseline alone cannot tell a 0.3 %-per-step leak apart from normal sender drift; Model B picks up another 15 percentage points of recall from the driving-zone features (sustained high-RPM regimes overlap with the leak windows), but still leaves 34 % of the labelled rows uncaught; Model C closes the gap to 7 % residual missed rows because the per-asset deviation features turn a sub-noise per-step delta into a clearly anomalous deviation from the truck-driver-route consumption profile.
The six false negatives in Model C are the trailing rows of two long gradual-leak windows where the per-step delta drops below the slosh-suppression baseline once the leak has been running for ten or more samples. The leak itself is detected — every one of those trips has earlier rows above the threshold and is flagged at trip-level — so the residual missed rows are a row-granularity artefact of the detector decaying to sub-noise sensitivity inside a sustained leak, not a detection failure. The six false positives are spread across four clean trips (one or two rows each) where the detector flagged a brief over-baseline excursion during acceleration after a refuel-window slosh recovery; the deterministic contextual gate in the backend rejects this pattern in production, and the live-deployment alert rate in §6.4.6 shows the operational effect of that gate.

The static threshold reaches the precision target trivially because a 1 % single-sample drop is by construction a sudden event, but it covers only 7.1 % of the labelled positives — it sees the two sudden-drop events and four of the abnormal-rapid-decrease events and misses every gradual-leak row, because gradual leaks are designed to remain below the 1 %-per-step threshold. It is therefore a one-anomaly-class detector and is reported here primarily as the lower-bound reference for what a no-machine-learning solution looks like.
The per-truck IQR on fuel-per-kilometre crosses the recall target but misses the precision target (76.7 %) because the Tukey 1.5× IQR bound flags many legitimate-but-statistically-rare driving moments (heavy acceleration on a clean trip, brief idle, refuel slosh) on top of the true anomalous rows. The per-truck IQR on fuel delta, by contrast, is a strong baseline — 97.7 % precision, 100 % recall, 0.36 % false-positive rate — and outperforms the Isolation Forest on row-level point estimates against this specific evaluation set.
The choice to deploy the Isolation Forest as the production detector is therefore not purely a precision-and-recall argument; it is a coverage-and-interpretability argument. All three injected anomaly classes (sudden, gradual, abnormal-rapid-decrease) produce abnormally large or persistent fuel-delta values, which is exactly the single-axis signal the IQR rule reads. The Isolation Forest's multivariate view does not pay off on a single-axis anomaly distribution, but it provides two operational properties the IQR baseline cannot: (a) coverage of anomalies that do not perturb fuel-delta in obvious ways — siphoning during a refuel cycle, sender-spoof flat-line readings, and slow drips that stay below the noise floor per-step but produce a clear per-asset baseline deviation — and (b) a multivariate feature-importance trace that lets the operator-facing dashboard explain why a row was flagged. The Isolation Forest is therefore retained as the production detector, the per-truck-IQR-on-fuel-delta rule is retained as a documented baseline and a parallel sanity-check tripwire, and the Local Outlier Factor is documented as a future-work density-based ensemble companion.

<Heading2> 6.4.9 Discussion
The machine-learning subsystem meets every deliverable required by SO4 on the intended evaluation partition. The production detector — Isolation Forest Model C operating on a 33-feature engineered vector — achieves 92.9 % precision, 92.9 % recall, and 1.09 % false-positive rate under strict leave-one-trip-out cross-validation, with trip-level recall of six out of six on the six injected anomalous trips. The trip-gated Hybrid Intersection achieves 97.4 % precision at the cost of 3.5 percentage points of row-level recall, and is retained as the recommended high-confidence alert channel for the operator-facing dashboard. The detector-versus-baseline analysis shows that a per-truck IQR rule on fuel delta is competitive on this evaluation set, but the Isolation Forest is preferred for production because it generalises to anomaly modes outside the three injected classes and supplies the multivariate feature-importance trace required for operator-facing alert explanations. The live-deployment audit over 37.7 days and 11,741 production telemetry rows shows the system operating well inside the SO4 false-positive design target after the deterministic gate has filtered the generous model-layer flags (0.72 % effective alert rate against a 10 % ceiling), with 85.9 % of surfaced alerts resolved closed-loop by an administrator. The extended controlled-environment validation suite provides honest evidence of the model's coverage on security-relevant classes outside the original evaluation domain: clean catches on moving-vehicle theft and short-window depot siphoning, and a documented blind spot on parked-overnight siphoning that motivates the future-work stationary-fuel-drop detector.

<Heading2> 6.5.7 Cross-Interface Synchronisation Evidence
The three user-facing surfaces — embedded human–machine interface, mobile companion, and administrative dashboard — are reconciled through the backend so the driver, the mobile companion, and the fleet manager always see the same trip state. The evidence for this synchronisation is the consistency of the trip-state badges, the live counters, and the alert events across the three surfaces during a single trip session.
For the active trip captured in Figure 6.22, the in-truck human–machine interface reads "active" with a 01:15:12 driving-time counter and a CELL / WiFi / LoRa connectivity badge. The corresponding mobile-companion screen (Figure 6.10 in §6.3) reads "ACTIVE" with the same elapsed counter, and the dashboard fleet-status card for the same truck (Figure 6.25) reads "Active Trip — 0 km/h" with the same mileage and rest-countdown values. When the driver initiates a rest break, the in-truck human–machine interface flips to the "Driver Resting" state in Figure 6.23, the mobile companion flips to the RESTING state in Figure 6.12 (§6.3), and the dashboard fleet-status card surfaces the paused-state badge — all three transitions are observed within the polling cadence of the slowest surface (the dashboard polls at 15 s intervals; the mobile companion polls at 5 s intervals; the in-truck device transitions immediately on the physical button press and propagates upward through the event endpoint).
The same synchronisation is observed for the alert-resolution path. When an administrator resolves an alert through the dashboard alerts page (Figure 6.26), the resolution propagates back to the truck-status card and to the relevant trip's activity timeline on the per-trip detail page (Figure 6.30) without manual refresh on either surface.
<Heading2> 6.5.8 Discussion
The alert system, the embedded human–machine interface, and the administrative dashboard together evidence every deliverable required by SO5. The rule engine triggers correctly on the four implemented alert classes and the live deployment produced 100 alerts over 37.7 days with a 95 % resolution rate, exceeding the operational closed-loop target. The embedded human–machine interface operates as intended across the full trip-state machine (login, active, rest, resume, end) with audio and visual indication on the rest path, and is mounted operationally in the cab alongside the manufacturer instrument cluster. The administrative dashboard exposes the seven required surfaces — live map, alerts management, telemetry logs, trip history, analytics, fleet settings, and user management — and propagates trip-state transitions and alert resolutions across the three concurrent client interfaces within the slowest polling cadence. The Administrator Acceptance Survey (n = 4) returned an aggregate System Usability Scale score of 81.9, placing the dashboard inside the "excellent" usability band, and the custom acceptance questions on fuel-anomaly utility, cross-interface synchronisation, and willingness-to-recommend scored at or above 4.5 on the 5-point Likert scale. Two safety alerts (lane departure, fatigue and drowsiness) remain out of scope because they require additional sensors (camera, inertial measurement unit, face-detection model) that fall outside the SO5 envelope and are documented as future work.
